The actual bar

Every track's brief is a measurement demand in disguise

Read the four bars back to back. “Measured precision and recall on a held-out test set.” “Honest metrics including false-positive cost.” “Show measured money recovered across a batch.” “One cherry-picked match proves nothing.”

None of those are feature requests. They are all the same request, repeated four times: prove your system works on data that could have embarrassed it. Nearly every submission will clear the build bar. The filter is here.

And the problem is real: if you generate the data and then grade yourself on it, you have proved nothing except that your generator and your model agree. There are exactly four ways out of that, and they are not equally available across the four tracks.

The escape routes

Four ways to earn a number you didn't author

Strongest

Third-party randomisation

Public data where somebody else ran the randomised trial. Treatment was assigned by coin flip, by them, before you existed. The counterfactual is identifiable and the experiment is not yours to bias.

Strong

Real labels, temporal split

Real ground-truth labels you didn't write, tested forward in time rather than on a random shuffle. Chronology is what stops the future leaking into the past.

Strong

Off-policy evaluation

Score a policy you have never deployed using data logged under a different one, via importance weighting. The standard way to grade an agent's decisions before letting it touch money.

Weak alone

Simulator with published DGP

Synthetic data whose generating process you disclose in full. Worthless as sole evidence, genuinely valuable stacked on top of the others — it's the only way to know true per-unit effects.

Findings

The four tracks, ranked by how defensible their numbers can be

1

Track 03 — AI Revenue Recovery

Solved

This one isn't close. Uplift modelling — estimating the incremental effect of an intervention rather than the raw outcome — comes with large, real, publicly released randomised trials.

13,979,592 Rows in the Criteo-UPLIFT benchmark: a genuine advertising incrementality trial with randomised treatment at p = 0.85, released at AdKDD 2018 and available through sklift.datasets.fetch_criteo. Hillstrom MineThatData adds a real 64,000-customer, three-arm email RCT.

Because Criteo randomised the treatment, the counterfactual is identifiable at population level, and you can compute a true Qini coefficient and AUUC on a held-out slice of an experiment you had no hand in running. Then apply the same validated estimator to a payments simulator and score the resulting policy with off-policy evaluation. Two independent evidence layers, neither self-authored.

The segmentation is also instantly legible in a five-minute pitch: Persuadables, Sure Things, Lost Causes, and Sleeping Dogs — customers your intervention actively drives away. Nobody at this hackathon will model negative treatment effects, and it is precisely what the brief means by false-positive cost.

Verdict: the only track where the honest number is also the impressive one.

2

Track 02 — AI Risk Manager

Real, but unflattering

Real labelled data is abundant — IEEE-CIS carries 590,540 transactions at roughly 3.5% fraud. The brief's request for precision and recall on a held-out set is directly satisfiable. The rigour lives entirely in the traps.

0.925 → 0.537 Average precision on IEEE-CIS, moving from a random validation split to a true forward-in-time test period. ROC-AUC falls 0.977 → 0.853 over the same change. Random splits leak the future into the past; the collapse is what honesty costs.

Three non-negotiables: split temporally rather than randomly, report PR-AUC rather than ROC-AUC under this imbalance, and price false positives and false negatives separately — blocking a good customer and waving through fraud are not symmetric losses.

The genuinely under-known angle is verification latency. In production you don't get labels on time: investigators check a handful quickly, the rest arrive days later as chargebacks. Dal Pozzolo et al. show the right response is training separate classifiers on investigator feedback and on delayed labels, then aggregating — not pooling them. Almost nobody models this. On the algorithm side, FRAUDAR (KDD 2016 Best Paper) does camouflage-resistant dense-subgraph detection with provable bounds on fraudster effectiveness, mapping cleanly onto the abuse-ring brief.

Verdict: rigour is available, but it means publishing numbers that look worse than your competitors' dishonest ones.

3

Track 04 — AI Finance Controller

No benchmark for the real task

There is no public invoice-to-payment reconciliation dataset with gold-standard matches. That data is commercially sensitive and nobody releases it. This is a genuine, unfixable gap in the track.

The proxies are a step removed but real: Magellan, DeepMatcher and the Machamp generalised entity-matching benchmark all provide labelled match/no-match pairs with 9.4%–24.5% positive rates, and ERP-RiskBench offers procure-to-pay event logs covering two-way and three-way matching flows. You would validate the matcher on those, then run the full pipeline on a synthetic batch — the same two-layer structure as Track 03, but with a weaker outer layer.

The compensating strength is algorithmic taste. Many-to-many reconciliation with partial payments and netting is a set-partitioning problem, NP-hard in general and properly solved with integer programming, not fuzzy string matching. Confine the LLM to parsing unstructured remittance advice and you have a textbook answer to the “where you chose not to use one” criterion. Infeasibility in the solver also hands you the honest exception list for free.

Verdict: excellent judgment story, thinner external validation.

4

Track 01 — AI Growth & Agentic Commerce

Structurally unmeasurable

“Grow the merchant's revenue” cannot be evidenced without buyers, and no public randomised trial of AI-buyer commerce exists. You would be asserting value, not measuring it.

There is one credible framework. τ-bench grades conversational tool-using agents by comparing the final database state against an annotated goal state — not by having a language model judge the transcript — and introduces pass^k: run the same task k independent times and measure how often every run succeeds.

pass^8 < 25% GPT-4o on τ-bench retail, against under 50% on single-attempt success. A devastating and highly quotable statistic about how unreliable commerce agents actually are once you stop measuring them once.

You could absolutely build a τ-bench-style harness over a checkout agent and be rigorous. But note what you'd be proving: reliability, not revenue. You would be answering a question the track didn't ask, and a sharp judge will say so.

Verdict: the trendiest track and the one where honest evidence is hardest to produce.

Recommended build · Track 03

Three evidence layers that fail in different ways

The design principle: use estimators and datasets whose weaknesses don't overlap. Agreement between methods that could each be wrong for unrelated reasons is what converts a demo into evidence.

Layer A

External validity

Fit the uplift estimator on Criteo-UPLIFT and Hillstrom and report Qini and AUUC against published baselines. Real randomised data, real experiment, run by someone else. This proves the pipeline works on data you did not create — and it is the layer that pre-empts the “you made this up” objection before it's raised.

Layer B

Off-policy evaluation

Log simulator actions under a stochastic behaviour policy with known propensities, then score your target policy with IPS, self-normalised IPS and doubly robust estimators. Three estimators that fail under different conditions, so convergence between them is informative. Report confidence intervals, not point estimates.

Layer C

Simulator with disclosed generating process

State plainly that it's synthetic, publish the DGP, name the confounding you injected, and hold out a control arm you never touch. Ground-truth per-unit treatment effects are known by construction here, which lets you report estimator error directly — something no real dataset permits. The disclosure is the credibility, not a weakness to bury.

The move almost nobody makes

Ship a passing placebo test

Run your entire pipeline end to end on data where the treatment has zero true effect by construction. If the system still reports money recovered, your evaluation is broken and you have just found out cheaply.

Publishing a negative control that passes is close to unanswerable. It demonstrates that you understand your own measurement can lie to you, which is a strictly higher-order claim than any accuracy number, and it takes about an hour once the harness exists.

Judging criterion · AI judgment

Where the LLM belongs, and where it does not

The rubric asks for “the right tool in the right place, and where you chose not to use one.” That second clause is unusual and it is worth an explicit section in your README, because the default submission will be an LLM wrapped around everything.

Deliberately not an LLM
  • Degradation detectionWilson-interval significance testing on segment success rates. This is hypothesis testing; a language model cannot do it correctly and shouldn't be asked to.
  • Uplift estimationT-learner or X-learner via causalml. Treatment-effect estimation has a correct estimator class with known properties. Using a chat model here would be malpractice.
  • Action selectionA deterministic policy engine over uplift, margin, consent state and caps. Anything that moves money must be reproducible, auditable, and identical on re-run.
Genuinely an LLM
  • Root-cause narrativeTurning segment statistics into an explanation a merchant can act on. Constrained to a fixed cause enum so it cannot invent a diagnosis.
  • Customer message copyHinglish and English recovery messaging, tone-matched to the failure reason. Language is where the real ambiguity lives.
  • Unstructured parsingReading messy failure reasons, bank strings and free-text remittance notes into structured fields. Classic extraction work with no deterministic alternative.
One more thing

The competitive trap in Track 03

Razorpay already ships Optimizer, an ML payment router working over 150 parameters and 600 million data points, claiming up to 10% success-rate lift. Their Subscriptions product already does smart retry, claiming 57% recovery of failed attempts.

If you build “smart retry” or “smart routing,” you are rebuilding their shipped product, worse, in a week. That is the most likely way a Track 03 entry dies.

But the same research points at the gap. Razorpay published a blog post titled “How do you know that your Payments Router is actually creating value for your business?” — and shipped an entire performance report to answer it. That is not a routing problem. It is a causal measurement problem, and it is unsolved on their own product surface.

So don't compete on recovering more. Compete on proving the recovery was caused by you.
